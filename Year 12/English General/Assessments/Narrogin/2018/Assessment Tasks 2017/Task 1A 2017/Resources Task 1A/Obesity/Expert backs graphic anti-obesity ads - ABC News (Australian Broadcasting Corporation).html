Expert backs graphic anti-obesity ads

By Monique Ross

Updated 24 Jul 2012, 6:00pmTue 24 Jul 2012, 6:00pm

Photo: One of the controversial LiveLighter ads (www.livelighter.com.au)
Related Story: West Australians fatter but smoking less
Related Story: You are what your mum ate: obesity research
Related Story: Survey finds support for fast food ad ban
Related Story: Obesity weighing down mine industry
Map: WA

A health expert has denied claims that a series of advertisements which aim to tackle obesity in Western Australia are instead encouraging eating disorders.

Around 60 per cent of West Australians are classed as overweight or obese, higher than the national average.

The State Government-funded LiveLighter campaign, developed with the Heart Foundation and the Cancer Council, journeys inside the body to show the impact of an unhealthy diet.

Echoing the graphic anti-smoking ads rolled out across the country, one ad shows fat built up around the internal organs of a "moderately overweight" man.

"When you eat more than you need to and aren't as active as you should be, fat doesn't just build up around your waist," the ad states.

"A grabbable gut on the outside is a sure sign there's toxic fat on the inside."

The controversial advertisements have attracted a mixed response. Among those rallying against them is Lydia Jade Turner, who runs a private eating disorder clinic in Sydney.

She has started an online petition to have the $9 million campaign pulled.

"With this subjective hand grab of the gut, we can imagine young people imagining a layer of fat there that perhaps isn't there, and engaging in harmful weight loss behaviours in a bid to appear healthy," she told triple j's Hack.

"I treat people with eating disorders, and that's why I speak out about this campaign."

Video: Professor Amanda Sainsbury-Salis talks to ABC News Breakfast (ABC News)

But Professor Amanda Sainsbury-Salis, an expert on obesity and eating disorders with Sydney University's Boden Institute, says the ads take a responsible approach.

"In fact the ads depict very sensible, healthy lifestyle options for anyone of any age and any body weight," she told ABC News Breakfast.

"This includes, for example, don't stand in front of the fridge eating pizza. Another message in the ads is if you can walk to the shops, do so, instead of driving. Another message is don't pick up two big bags of chips in the supermarket and buy them because they're two for the price of one.

"These are behaviours that are very healthful and which will promote healthy body weight, healthy metabolic profile in all people.

"So there's nothing in the ads in terms of weight management behaviour that would promote or which is known to promote or which is known to promote eating disorders."

Professor Sainsbury-Salis acknowledges it is important to be cautious about the images portrayed in public health messages.

"An area of ongoing research [is] the effect of public health campaigns on obesity, on young people of all ages and the development of eating disorders," she said.

But she says the ads will play a valuable role in curbing obesity.

"We know that in certain circumstances fear is a greater motivator than the desire for pleasurable experiences and especially in terms of prevention of obesity," she said.

"[But] this is the truth. The fat around the internal organs is toxic, it does contribute to diseases such as certain cancers, diabetes, heart disease.

"So, you know, it's telling it like it is."
